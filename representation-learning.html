Representation learning

Representation learning transforms observations into features for later analysis. The aim is not simply to use fewer features, but to organise information in a form suited to the task.

Purpose of a representation

The performance of a machine-learning method depends on the representation supplied to it. Let X denote an input space containing observations such as feature vectors, time series or images. A feature transformation maps an observation x to a representation z in a feature space Z.

z=φ(x),x∈𝒳,z∈𝒵

The transformation can be designed manually through feature engineering or learned from the observations. A transformation learned from data is referred to as representation learning or feature learning [2].

Representation learning is often associated with dimensionality reduction, but fewer dimensions are not required. A learned representation can contain fewer, the same number of or more dimensions than the input. The undercomplete autoencoder in the thesis deliberately produces a lower-dimensional representation.

A useful representation makes the next task easier. A compact feature vector is not necessarily suitable for prediction or clustering: the relevant information must remain accessible to the algorithm that uses it. A representation that supports a linear classifier may not provide the distance relationships needed by a clustering algorithm [1].

Invariant features

An invariant representation remains unchanged under a specified transformation. In practice, a model may instead reduce sensitivity to that change. For object identification, changes in lighting or orientation need not change the predicted identity. A representation used for that task can therefore suppress sensitivity to those changes [1].

Invariance is task-dependent. Lighting may be irrelevant to identifying an object but essential to estimating illumination. Removing sensitivity to a factor can therefore help one task while discarding information needed for another.

Environmental regionalisation does not have one response variable that determines which variation is irrelevant. Temperature, precipitation, humidity, wind and their temporal patterns can all distinguish locations. The autoencoder was therefore not trained to remove variation from any environmental channel.

Disentanglement

Disentanglement aims to represent different factors of variation separately. An object representation could separate identity, orientation and lighting, while a video representation could separate identity, movement and camera position. Unlike invariance, which suppresses sensitivity to a selected factor, disentanglement aims to keep factors separately accessible for different tasks [1].

A standard reconstruction objective does not automatically produce disentangled dimensions. Even when specialised objectives are used, one learned dimension is not guaranteed to correspond uniquely to one physical factor. Interpretable changes in a latent direction also do not, by themselves, establish a causal mechanism [3].

The autoencoder in the thesis had no disentanglement objective. Information about one environmental factor could consequently be distributed across several embedding dimensions.

Distributed representations

A distributed representation describes an observation through a combination of features rather than a single category identifier. Features can be reused across observations, allowing shared attributes to support generalisation to new combinations. For example, colour, shape and size can describe many objects without requiring a separate feature for every possible object [1].

Distributed does not mean disentangled. A learned code can use several features together without those features having separate, human-readable meanings. The labelled attributes above illustrate combination and reuse, not a guarantee about the features learned by a neural network.

The training objective determines what matters

A training objective determines which errors the model is encouraged to reduce. With limited representational capacity, reducing average reconstruction error can favour prominent patterns over small details that matter for a later task. An example in Chapter 15 shows an autoencoder reconstruction that omitted a small ping-pong ball even though the ball was relevant to the robotics task [1], Figure 15.5.

For environmental observations, the same general concern motivates checking individual variables and events rather than relying on one average reconstruction score. Weighting reconstruction terms or adding a task-specific objective changes the training priorities, but any improvement must be assessed against the intended use of the features.

Reusing and sharing representations

A representation learned in one setting may contain features useful in another. Transfer learning reuses learned information for another setting or task, either by keeping an encoder fixed or by fine-tuning its parameters. Domain adaptation addresses changes in the input distribution for a related task [1].

  • Multitask learning: several tasks use shared features while retaining task-specific outputs.
  • Multimodal learning: separate encoders process different input types, and learning connects their representations.
  • Transfer learning: a previously learned representation provides a starting point for a new application.

As a conceptual environmental example, one representation could support clustering and a separate prediction model. A multimodal design could connect climate sequences with satellite images. These are possible designs, not additional experiments performed in the thesis.

Reuse is most promising when tasks or domains share relevant factors. Useful transfer is not automatic, so performance must be evaluated on the new task or domain [1].

Learning assumptions

Representation learning is guided by modelling choices. Architecture, objectives and regularisation encode assumptions about the patterns worth learning. Examples include smoothness, sparsity and temporal coherence: nearby inputs may have related outputs, only a subset of features may be active, or underlying factors may change slowly over time [1], Section 15.6.

The manifold hypothesis does not itself imply naturally separated clusters. A dataset can follow one connected manifold while varying gradually along it. Treating observations as distinct groups requires an additional assumption or an application-dependent partition. Natural clustering is listed separately from the manifold assumption in Chapter 15 [1].

Role in environmental regionalisation

The representation connects the environmental observations to the clustering algorithm. The complete relationship is expressed as

{x}i=1n→φ{z}i=1n→𝒜{c}i=1n

Here, 𝒜 is the clustering procedure and ci is the environmental region assigned to site i. The transformation affects the regionalisation because the clustering algorithm has access only to the information and geometry present in the learned feature space.

Evaluation of a learned representation

The objective of the thesis was to obtain a compact representation that retained information from the multivariate climate series and arranged locations in a form suitable for comparison and clustering. Reconstruction error alone could not establish this property. A model could reconstruct dominant or smoothly varying channels while retaining less information about other environmental signals.

The final embedding was therefore evaluated through environmental-information recovery and geometry diagnostics. The recovery analysis tested whether environmental features remained predictable from the embedding. The geometry analysis tested whether distances between nearby embeddings remained meaningful after decoding.
